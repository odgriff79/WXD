### Post 1 (296 chars)
🧵 Why do winter forecasts so often flip from "cold/blocked" to "mild/zonal" as we get closer?

It's not just bad luck — there's a well-documented systematic bias in weather models that affects this exact scenario.

Here's what the research says...

## Post 2 (299 chars)
Models consistently show an "overly progressive Atlantic" bias in winter — they favour westerly flow pushing through, and struggle to establish or maintain blocking high pressure over Scandinavia and Europe.

The result? Blocked patterns often get "washed out" in the forecast.

## Post 3 (298 chars)
How bad is it? Research shows climate models underestimate Euro-Atlantic winter blocking by 30-80% depending on region.

The bias is worst over the British Isles/North Sea — one study found models ~65% lower than observed blocking frequency there.

## Post 4 (295 chars)
The underlying issue: models produce storm tracks that are "too zonal and too equatorward" — a bias that's persisted for decades across multiple model generations (CMIP3→CMIP6).

Higher resolution helps, but doesn't fully fix it.

## Post 5 (294 chars)
Why it matters for forecasting: transitions from zonal to blocked regimes are where the biggest "forecast busts" occur.

Models handle maintaining an existing pattern better than capturing the onset of blocking — exactly when you need the skill most.

Key refs if you want to dig deeper:

## Gemini's Thread

### Post 1/6
Ever feel like the winter "Easterly" you're tracking on the models keeps getting pushed back? 🕰️ It's not just bad luck. There is a documented "zonal bias" in NWP models where they struggle to transition from a progressive Atlantic flow to a blocked state over Europe. Let's look at the science. 🧵 #Meteorology #WeatherModel #WinterWeather

### Post 2/6
At the heart of the issue is the "Too Zonal and Too Equatorward" storm-track bias. Many models tend to keep the North Atlantic jet stream too straight and too far south, effectively "shoving" weather systems across Europe rather than allowing them to buckle and form high-pressure blocks.

### Post 3/6
Why does this happen? Research suggests a few "usual suspects":
• Orography: Poor representation of mountain drag can lead to an overly fast jet.
• Moist Processes: Errors in how models handle latent heat in clouds can weaken the Rossby waves needed to start a block.
• Resolution: Lower-res models often underestimate blocking frequency by 30-50%.

### Post 4/6
Even the "gold standard" ECMWF isn't immune. Technical bulletins highlight a "chronic underestimation" of European blocking in seasonal systems. Models are statistically "too keen" to stay in a +NAO (zonal) state, making them slow to catch the onset of a Scandinavian or Greenland High.

### Post 5/6
This creates a "forecast bust" cycle: models show a block at Day 10, but as the date nears, the Atlantic "zips" through it. Recent studies in 2025 show that even as models improve, they still underestimate blocking frequency by up to 80% in some winter scenarios. 📉

### Post 6/6
The takeaway? When you see a "zonal" look on the 12z GFS or ECMWF, remember the bias: models are structurally "hard-wired" to favor the Atlantic. It often takes a major signal to break that progressive momentum. ❄️🛡️

Closely linked is the long-standing "too zonal / too equatorward" North Atlantic storm-track bias. When storm tracks are displaced south and overly progressive, models struggle to maintain Rossby-wave breaking needed for sustained European blocking.

Research highlights contributing factors:
• resolution limits
• orographic drag representation
• moist vs dry process errors
• SST and jet-latitude biases
These collectively bias models toward NAO+-like solutions and against Scandinavian/Greenland blocks.

Operational centres acknowledge this. ECMWF documentation and QJRMS papers explicitly discuss systematic under-representation of winter blocking and why regime-based tools (NAO/BLO frameworks) are needed to compensate for flow-dependent bias.
